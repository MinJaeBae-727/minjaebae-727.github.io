Biography

Hello, I am Min Jae Bae, a plant molecular biologist studying how the chromatin-modifying protein HOS15 and the linker histone H1.3 allow plants to sense and survive cold stress. I trained in biomedical science and engineering at Konkuk University, where I began my research career studying HOS15, a substrate receptor of the DDB1–CUL4 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, and its functional interplay with the histone deacetylase HD2C in cold stress signaling.

During my graduate training, I showed that HOS15-mediated changes in histone acetylation and methylation reshape chromatin states and drive stress-responsive transcriptional reprogramming in Arabidopsis thaliana. At the protein level, I dissected the structure–function relationship of HOS15 through single-amino-acid mutagenesis of its LisH motif to map residues required for its activity and conformation, and I characterized the intrinsically disordered region (IDR) within its coiled-coil domain. Through this work, I developed strong expertise in domain-level protein biochemistry and its connection to chromatin regulation.

Since starting my Ph.D. in 2021, I have expanded my research toward the linker histone H1.3 and its role in chromatin plasticity under cold stress. To connect these molecular changes to global stress responses, I combine NicE-seq chromatin accessibility profiling, RNA-seq, and LC-MS-based proteomics of hos15 and h1 mutants before and after cold, integrating domain-level biochemistry with genome- and proteome-wide analysis to examine how H1.3 degradation reshapes chromatin organization and accessibility.

Looking forward, I aim to investigate how these chromatin-level mechanisms contribute to stress resilience and long-term adaptation in the context of climate change and extreme environments, including spaceflight-relevant conditions. Ultimately, my goal is to integrate protein structure–function studies, proteomics, and chromatin-level analysis to identify the mechanistic drivers of plant stress tolerance and to provide a molecular foundation for engineering climate-resilient crops.

Research Focus

1. Linker Histone H1.3 and Chromatin Decondensation Under Cold Stress

Using h1 and hos15 mutants, GFP-tagged H1 variants, confocal imaging, NicE-seq chromatin accessibility profiling, RNA-seq, and LC-MS proteomics, I study how the stress-inducible linker histone H1.3 shapes chromatin and the cold response. I found that cold lowers H1 protein in a HOS15- and proteasome-dependent manner and that H1.3 is the variant that is lost. h1.3 mutants are more freezing tolerant, have fewer and less condensed chromocenters, resembling cold-treated wild-type nuclei, and show increased accessibility mainly in pericentromeric heterochromatin. Loss of H1.3 leaves the core CBF–COR cold program intact but pre-activates defense- and lignin-related genes and allows cold induction of heat-shock and antioxidant genes.

2. HOS15 as a Substrate-Recruiting Subunit of a Ubiquitin E3 Ligase

I study HOS15 as a component of a ubiquitin E3 ligase complex that integrates cold stress signaling with chromatin modification and alternative splicing. HOS15 cooperates with HDACs and other chromatin factors to fine-tune gene expression, flowering time, and stress acclimation, establishing HOS15-centered complexes as key regulatory hubs in plant cold stress responses. In my Ph.D. work, I showed that HOS15 forms homodimers through its N-terminal LisH motif and that this dimerization is required for cold-induced ubiquitination and degradation of HD2C and for freezing tolerance.

3. Applied Biotechnology & Bioenergy

Beyond fundamental chromatin biology, I have contributed to patent applications on vector and strain engineering for improved cellulose, oil, and sucrose accumulation, and enhanced ethanol fermentation — translating mechanistic plant biology into applied biotechnology.
