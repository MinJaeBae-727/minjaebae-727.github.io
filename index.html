Biography

Hello, I am Minjae Bae, a plant molecular biologist studying how chromatin structure and histone dynamics allow plants to survive cold stress. I trained in biomedical science and engineering at Konkuk University, where I began my research career studying HOS15, a substrate receptor of the CRL4 ubiquitin ligase complex, and its role in cold stress signaling.

During my Ph.D., I have investigated how the linker histone H1.3 controls nucleosome organization and chromatin accessibility under low temperature, using CRISPR-based genetics, ChIP-seq, RNA-seq, and confocal imaging. My work shows that H1 degradation and redistribution are tightly coupled to chromatin plasticity and cold tolerance, and that HOS15-centered complexes act as regulatory hubs coordinating chromatin modification with stress acclimation.

Alongside mechanistic wet-lab work, I have developed strong expertise in integrating multi-omics data — RNA-seq, LC-MS proteomics, and MOFA/MOFA2-based multi-omics factor analysis — to build a systems-level picture of epigenetic regulation during stress adaptation.

Research Focus

1. Linker Histone H1 and Chromatin Accessibility Under Cold Stress

Using CRISPR-based genetics, ChIP-seq, RNA-seq, and confocal imaging, I dissect how H1 variants modulate nucleosome organization and stress-responsive gene expression under low temperature. My work shows that H1 degradation and redistribution are tightly coupled to chromatin plasticity and cold tolerance, providing a mechanistic framework for dynamic chromatin remodeling during stress.

2. HOS15 as a Substrate-Recruiting Subunit of a Ubiquitin E3 Ligase

I study HOS15 as a component of a ubiquitin E3 ligase complex that integrates cold stress signaling with chromatin modification and alternative splicing. HOS15 cooperates with HDACs and other chromatin factors to fine-tune gene expression, flowering time, and stress acclimation, establishing HOS15-centered complexes as key regulatory hubs in plant cold stress responses.

3. Applied Biotechnology & Bioenergy

Beyond fundamental chromatin biology, I have contributed to five patent applications on vector and strain engineering for improved cellulose, oil, and sucrose accumulation, and enhanced ethanol fermentation — translating mechanistic plant biology into applied biotechnology.
